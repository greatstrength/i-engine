# Hexagram 29 - The Abysmal (K'an)

## Overview

### Summary
The trigram of Water shows a firm line plunged amidst two yielding lines, portraying the image of water rushing through a ravine. Such a situation is inherently dangerous, where the best approach is to go with the flow, filling all gaps and not lingering in peril. In nature, this represents the life-giving rains, rivers, and lakes. In human terms, Water symbolizes the heart or the expression of the Soul, the vital essence within our physical form.

### Judgement
The Abysmal repeated. If you are sincere, you have success in your heart, and whatever you do succeeds.

Water is inherently unstable and can be dangerous when moving in large volumes, as evidenced by the devastation caused by floods and storms in rivers and oceans. Historically, rulers have employed these natural barriers as defenses against adversaries. Similarly, individuals might use the perception of danger to protect themselves from potential threats.

### The Image
Water flows on uninterruptedly and reaches its goal: the image of the Abysmal repeated. Thus the superior man walks in lasting virtue and carries on the business of teaching.

When this trigram is doubled, it signifies repeated encounters with danger. Facing these challenges repeatedly leads to the fortification of one's character through experiential wisdom. This resilience is essential for anyone aiming to undertake significant endeavors. Understanding the value of repeated exposure to danger also underscores the need for continuous learning and deep engagement with study. Only by fully immersing oneself in learning can one truly grasp and retain knowledge, much like water permeates and becomes one with the earth.

## Changing Lines

### Six in First
Repetition of the Abysmal. In the abyss one falls into a pit. Misfortune.

When one "falls into a pit" within the context of repeated dangers or challenges, they become so accustomed to peril that they might even seek it out, finding the tranquility of peace uncomfortable or un-stimulating. This line suggests that such an individual has internalized the dangers they face to such an extent that they now embody or prefer the chaos and misfortune over stability, leading to further misfortune as they are unable or unwilling to adapt to or appreciate a more peaceful existence.

### Nine in Second
The abyss is dangerous. One should strive to attain small things only.

In the face of profound peril, akin to navigating through a treacherous abyss or surviving a tempest at sea, the focus should be on maintaining one's position or safety rather than on achieving grand ambitions. This line advises that in such times, the goal should be modest—to keep oneself and what one is responsible for (like a ship and its crew) intact and afloat. Success in this context is measured by survival and small, essential accomplishments, recognizing that the current conditions are not conducive to expansive or risky endeavors.

### Six in Third
Forward and backward, abyss on abyss. In danger like this, pause at first and wait, otherwise you will fall into a pit in the abyss. Do not act in this way.

Surrounded by dangers from every direction, this line counsels patience and stillness over action. In such an overwhelming scenario, where peril seems inescapable, the wise approach is to wait for conditions to change or for an opportunity to emerge naturally. Acting out of impatience or insecurity can lead to further entanglement or disaster, likened here to falling into an even deeper pit within the abyss. This suggests that one should resist the urge to move or react hastily, as such actions could exacerbate the situation, leading to one's downfall.

### Six in Fourth
A jug of wine, a bowl of rice with it; earthen vessels simply handed in through the window. There is certainly no blame in this.

As previously noted, the trigram of Water symbolizes the Soul housed within the "earthen vessel" of the human body, paralleling how the Earth contains its waters—rivers, lakes, and oceans—which are both life-giving and potentially dangerous. This line uses the imagery of simple, earthly offerings passed through a window to suggest that, like these natural elements, the dangers and uncertainties we face are part of the natural order, operating according to the design of nature. These conditions, though perilous, are not to be blamed, as they are transient, moving through life's windows as part of the cycle of existence. One is encouraged to accept danger as an inherent aspect of life that evolves with time, reminding us to navigate these conditions without ascribing fault, understanding their place in the grand scheme of nature.

### Nine in Fifth
The abyss is not filled to overflowing, it is filled only to the rim. No blame.

This line advises that when confronting danger or challenges, one should engage only to the extent necessary—like filling an abyss just to its rim without causing an overflow. By doing exactly what the moment requires and no more, one avoids the repercussions that come from lingering in or exacerbating the danger. Overstepping or trying to do more than what's needed ("overflowing") can lead to unnecessary complications or further peril. Thus, there's no blame in managing the situation with precision and restraint, acknowledging the limits and working within them.

### Six in Sixth
Bound with cords and ropes, shut in between thorn-hedged prison walls: for three years one does not find the way. Misfortune.

This line depicts a scenario where one has been so thoroughly engulfed by danger or adversity that escape or progress seems impossible. The imagery of being bound and imprisoned by thorns symbolizes a state of entrapment due to one's own actions or the overwhelming nature of the situation. Here, the individual faces a prolonged period of misfortune, unable to find a path forward for a significant time, metaphorically "three years," which suggests a period of deep introspection, learning, or simply enduring the consequences of past decisions or circumstances before any chance of liberation or advancement can occur.
